Data Quality: The Hidden Challenge in AI Projects

Poor data quality kills more AI projects than bad algorithms. Practical strategies for data assessment, cleaning, and governance.

JT
JP Tibério
CEO & AI Strategist

Data quality issues derail AI projects more than any other single factor. In our consulting work, we estimate that poor data quality contributes to 60–70% of AI project failures—not bad algorithms, not insufficient compute, not poor model selection. The foundational problem that most AI initiatives underinvest in is the one that determines whether everything else can work.

The Data Quality Dimensions

Quality encompasses multiple dimensions that are each necessary and collectively insufficient:

Accuracy: Does the data correctly represent the real-world state it's meant to capture? A sensor miscalibrated by 3% produces beautifully clean data that encodes systematic lies.

Completeness: Are values present for required fields across all records? Missing data is rarely random—the pattern of missingness often carries information, and naively dropping incomplete records introduces selection bias.

Consistency: Are the same entities represented the same way across datasets? A customer who appears as "JP Tibério" in the CRM, "João P. Tiberio" in billing, and "TIBERIO" in shipping creates entity resolution problems that compound downstream.

Timeliness: Is data available when it's needed? A model that predicts customer churn from engagement data that's 3 days stale is working with expired signals.

Validity: Does data conform to domain constraints? A birth date of "1850-03-15" in a customer database, or a negative inventory count, indicates upstream system problems.

Uniqueness: Are records deduplicated? Duplicate training examples inflate dataset size and can bias models toward patterns that appear in duplicated records.

Why AI Amplifies Data Quality Problems

Traditional analytics can often tolerate moderate data quality issues—analysts apply judgment, outliers are footnoted, and dashboards include caveats. AI systems don't apply judgment; they learn from whatever patterns the data contains. Garbage in, garbage out—but amplified and automated.

Three specific ways AI makes data quality problems worse:

Models memorize errors: If 5% of your training labels are incorrect, the model learns to produce those incorrect outputs in similar contexts—at production scale and speed. Human error rates don't scale; model error rates do.

Bias propagates and compounds: If historical data encodes structural bias (certain demographic groups underrepresented, certain time periods overrepresented), models learn and reproduce that bias systematically. Unlike a human analyst who can be retrained, a model requires either new training data or explicit bias correction techniques.

Silent degradation: When data quality degrades in production—a schema change, a pipeline failure, a sensor drift—model outputs degrade silently unless explicit data quality monitoring is in place. The pipeline runs, predictions are generated, and the degradation can go undetected for weeks.

The Data Assessment Framework

Before any AI project begins, a structured data assessment should answer:

Availability and Coverage

What data exists? Where is it? Who owns it?

What is the historical depth? (Minimum 12–24 months for most seasonal patterns)

What is the coverage across the target population? (Is the training population representative of the inference population?)

Quality Profiling

Automated profiling tools (Great Expectations, dbt tests, Pandera) can characterize data quality systematically:

Null rates per column

Value distribution (min, max, mean, standard deviation, mode)

Referential integrity across joins

Temporal gaps in time-series data

This profiling should produce a Data Quality Score by dimension, with explicit pass/fail thresholds defined before any model training begins.

Label Quality (Supervised Learning)

For supervised ML, label quality is often the most overlooked dimension. Common issues:

Annotator disagreement: When multiple human labelers disagree on the same example, what is the ground truth? Inter-annotator agreement metrics (Cohen's kappa) should be measured and documented.

Concept drift in labels: Labels applied two years ago may reflect different business definitions or human standards than labels applied today.

Systematic labeling bias: Annotators who consistently differ from consensus should be identified and their labels reviewed.

Data Cleaning: What Actually Works

A hierarchy of data cleaning approaches, from least to most disruptive:

Fix the source: If data quality problems originate in upstream systems, fixing them at the source prevents the same issue recurring with every new data extraction. This is always preferable to downstream cleaning.

Imputation: For missing values, the right strategy depends on missingness mechanism. If missing at random—median or mean imputation with a missingness indicator feature. If missing not at random (the missingness itself carries information)—the indicator alone, without imputation.

Outlier treatment: Statistical outlier removal should be domain-validated. A "statistical outlier" in transaction data might be a legitimate large purchase or a genuine fraud case—both of which are exactly the signals the model needs to see.

Deduplication: Fuzzy matching (Levenshtein distance, phonetic similarity, ML-based entity matching) for record deduplication when exact matching fails.

Building Data Quality Into Pipelines

Reactive data cleaning (fixing quality issues after they occur) is significantly less effective than proactive data quality pipelines:

Schema validation at ingestion: Reject or quarantine records that fail schema validation rather than propagating corrupt data downstream

Statistical tests on each batch: Compare incoming data distribution to baseline using PSI or KS tests—flag significant drift before it corrupts model inputs

Data contracts between teams: Formal agreements specifying the schema, data types, completeness requirements, and update frequency of data shared between teams. Data contracts make quality expectations explicit and auditable.

Conclusion

Data quality is boring. It doesn't appear in research papers, doesn't generate conference talks, and doesn't fit in a 10-slide pitch deck. But it determines the ceiling of every AI project built on it. The organizations consistently delivering AI value have normalized data quality investment as a fundamental part of project budgeting—not an afterthought that gets addressed when the model underperforms. In our experience, spending 30% more time and budget on data quality before model development begins typically reduces total project cost by 40–60% by eliminating the expensive debugging cycles that poor data quality inevitably generates.
